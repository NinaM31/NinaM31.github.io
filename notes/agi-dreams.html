From AGI Dreams to Ultra-Slop Machines

What if, on the way to AGI, we are also building an ultra-slop machine that pollutes the data future models will depend on?

AGI Dreams

We dream about building AGI: systems that can reason, discover new ideas, challenge us, and perhaps eventually become more capable than us in many domains. But sometimes I look at the LLMs we are actually building and wonder if we are heading somewhere much less impressive.

Instead of machines that challenge our thinking, we have created systems that are extremely good at sounding confident, agreeing with us, and producing enormous amounts of content. And the more we use them to write, code, summarize, explain, and communicate for us, the more I wonder what happens to the originality and quality of the information environment they will eventually have to learn from.

Confidently Wrong

LLMs are useful, but they are also confidently wrong and manipulative. For simple, straightforward tasks, they are great. They can write boilerplate code, search through documentation, build quick proofs of concept, and fix my Slack messages so I sound confident and like I know exactly what is happening ƪ(˘⌣˘)ʃ

But when I use them for anything genuinely complicated, they often become annoying and sometimes actively slow me down.

I give an LLM a problem. It produces a solution. I review it, point out the issues, and ask it to regenerate the solution. I get "You're absolutely right..." and a new solution. I review that one, point out a new set of issues, and get another "You're absolutely right...". Now I'm stuck in an endless loop of reviewing different flavours of the same slop. At this point I am wrestling with an autocomplete engine that has been professionally trained to gaslight me.

Research on sycophancy has shown that language models can favor responses that align with a user's stated beliefs or preferences over responses that are more truthful or accurate.[1]

Silent Assumptions

The more dangerous problem is that LLMs make assumptions.

Suppose a mandatory deviceId is missing. Instead of stopping and saying, "This input is invalid" the AI may make the system more "robust" by assigning the customer identifier to the device identifier field, because apparently the best fallback for missing data is different data. ¯\_( ͡° ͜ʖ ͡°)_/¯ The code may look clean and even pass the tests, while quietly introducing a bug or data corruption.

Evaluations of AI-generated code have found that current models can produce plausible implementations while overlooking security problems or introducing vulnerable code. [2]

When the Requirement Is the Problem

LLMs also rarely challenge the requirements you give them.

“Wow. This requirement is dumb, it conflicts with the rest of the system, and it will become unmaintainable within six months.”

I have never had an LLM respond like that. Instead, it starts "Thinking...", "Pontificating...", "Simmering...", or whatever obnoxious term, notices the contradiction, and then tries to satisfy both sides with an increasingly desperate collection of if statements.

Slop at Scale

But my bigger concern goes beyond whether an LLM gives me sloppy code.

We are now producing AI-generated text, code, images, comments, summaries, reviews, documentation, and articles at a scale that would have been impossible a few years ago. A single bad answer is annoying. Millions of mediocre, confident, derivative answers being published back onto the internet is a different problem entirely.

I worry that we are slowly making it harder to distinguish something that came from actual human thought from something that was generated because someone pressed Enter. AI-generated content is already becoming a substantial fraction of newly published material online. What happens as synthetic content becomes an even larger part of the information future models learn from?

Recent estimates suggest that around half of newly published online articles may already be primarily AI-generated, while another large-scale study found that roughly 35% of newly published websites contain AI-generated or AI-assisted text. [3][4]

The Ultra-Slop Machine

In other words, LLMs may eventually become ultra-slop machines. ( ͡°( ͡° ͜ʖ( ͡° ͜ʖ ͡°)ʖ ͡°) ͡°)

Work on model collapse shows that indiscriminate recursive training on model-generated data can progressively damage the learned distribution, with low-probability parts of the original distribution disappearing first.[5]

Obviously, this does not mean synthetic data is automatically bad or that model collapse is inevitable. But it does make me think that human-generated, carefully verified, original data may become valuable as AI-generated content becomes more common.

Conclusion

LLMs are useful for searching, prototyping, boilerplate, rewriting messages, and—apparently— gaslighting. My objection is to treating fluent generation as evidence of understanding or reliability. The more capable these systems become, the more important it becomes to know when not to trust them.

Maybe we will eventually build AGI. But there is an uncomfortable possibility that, in trying to get there, we may have built an ultra-slop machine that keeps flooding the internet with more synthetic, repetitive content, slowly polluting the very data we may need to train the systems that are we hope will become AGI.
